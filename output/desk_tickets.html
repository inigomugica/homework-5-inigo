Reflection

Written from my own reflection on the problem 9 run. Evidence links jump to the Ticket 101, Ticket 102, Ticket 103 and Cash tabs on this page.

1. How would I evaluate the agents' performance on each ticket, and why?

Overall: mediocre. I understood the tickets and the business at a general level. Campus Customs pays its suppliers and its rent, checks inventory for every item it sells, and answers customers; in this build money only goes out (no sales are recorded). I also understood the database. What I underestimated, partly because I didn't want to overcomplicate things, is that you have to understand a business like this much more deeply to anticipate all the ways a ticket can go. The agents need enough tables to do the job without overcomplicating it, and they need guidance on how a ticket can unfold: which customer requests are routine, and what to do with the ones that aren't.

Looking at the Expected and Actual sections, apart from really nitpicky things, the agents the Boss called first and the delegations were exactly what I expected for all three tickets. But matching the plan isn't the same as a good design. The match shows that, while building this, Claude took my plan too literally, and my plan was flawed from the start: it was written for the "happy path" and never covered cash running out, rejections, or when a ticket counts as done. So even though actual matched expected, it shouldn't have, if the design had been better. That's one of the risks of building with AI: sometimes, instead of getting pushback, you just get the AI doing whatever you tell it.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

Working through all the tickets without a confusing sequence. The desk went ticket by ticket (101, then 102, then 103). When 101 asked for an approval, it wasn't resolved yet, so the desk moved on to 102 and only came back to 101 later. One agent holding the whole desk in one context wouldn't get lost as easily.

Evidence from the run:
  • Repeated reading: on every handoff, each agent called get_desk_briefing again (see the tool lists on each ticket tab).
  • Cost: multi-agent runs cost 41k–51k tokens each, while the single-agent closing run on 101 cost 8.6k.
  • Hard part was rules, not agents: the jobs were small (read a bill, request an approval, save a draft). The hard parts were rules: cash ordering, duplicates, when to close. Those were solved with MCP tools, not with more agents.
One agent with the same 9 tools would have been simpler, cheaper and easier to follow. Separate agents only pay off when the roles need different permissions or expertise.

4. Three new problems this agent team could solve with the tools we built

  1. A lot more inventory to track. check_stock_and_restock already works for any SKU and size. It shows what's on hand, what open tickets will use, what's on order, and which vendor can ship and when. Inventory can raise shortfall alerts across a much bigger catalogue (see 101 and 103).
  2. Several properties, and not knowing what's owed each month or whether it's paid. check_bills_and_cash reads every lease and invoice with its due date counted from desk.date_today. Facilities and Accounting can request each rent, and the pay tool moves each lease's next_due forward, exactly as it did for lease 1 on 102.
  3. A flood of customer requests once the page is popular. Customer Service can answer each one with save_customer_draft, using only facts from tools (stock, arrival date from lead_days, the discount floor). The drafts wait on the board for a human to send, as they did for Tauhid Zaman and the Yale AI Club.

5. Three new problems this agent team could not solve with the current tools, and what we'd need

ProblemWhy the team can't solve it todayTools and agents needed
1. Running out of money. We need to record money coming in, not just going out. Rule 6 means cash only goes out. That's why 103 got stuck: checking ended at $152.00, which can't cover a $264.00 restock, and nothing could bring cash in. A sales / receipts table, with MCP tools such as record_customer_payment and invoice_customer. A Sales / Receivables agent to bill customers and chase what they owe. Incoming money would still go through human approval and the audit trail.
2. Financial projections from history. Knowing ahead of time whether there'll be cash to restock, and whether we can accept an order before the stock exists, given the vendor's lead time. The tools only see today's balance and today's bills. No tool looks at past payments or upcoming dates, so on 103 the team couldn't say "approve the order once next week's sales arrive". A forecast_cash(days) tool built on payment history, due dates, lead times and expected sales, plus a check_order_feasibility(sku, qty, needed_by) tool. A Finance / Planning agent to rank purchases and tell Customer Service which delivery dates it can safely promise.
3. Talking to the Boss directly. The shop should be able to steer the team without anyone redesigning the tool. The dashboard only offers Engage, Run the desk, Approve and Reject. To change how a ticket was handled (for example, "offer the 8 hoodies now and the rest later" on 103), the code or prompts had to change. A chat panel and a route such as POST /api/boss/chat, so a person can talk with the Boss. MCP tools such as add_desk_note / set_ticket_instructions that store the human's guidance in desk.notes or on the ticket, so every agent reads it. This would make the most of the infrastructure we already have.
